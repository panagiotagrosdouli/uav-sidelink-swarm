\section{Results}
\subsection{Baseline interference and resource partitioning}
Under sequential-disjoint pairing with one shared resource and $G=0$, mean SINR decreases from $-0.49$~dB at $N=5$ to $-23.03$~dB at $N=100$. At $N=100$ (50 simultaneous directed links), first-TX success probability is 0.0128 and expected PHY goodput is 0.359~Mbps. A diagnostic using the explicit success-probability threshold 0.5 classifies 72.4\% of failed links at this operating point as aggregate-interference limited and 27.6\% as dominant-interferer limited; this is a heuristic diagnostic, not a causal channel classification.

Table~\ref{tab:n100} shows that frequency-resource separation can improve reliability despite reducing the PRBs available to each link. Goodput is non-monotonic: $R=4$ has higher mean SINR than $R=2$ but lower goodput. This reflects the joint effect of lower co-channel interference, changed conflict-graph assignments, and smaller per-link TBS; more resource groups do not guarantee more goodput. With $R=8,G=6$, first-TX success probability reaches 0.5292 and expected goodput 2.229~Mbps. Relative to $R=1,G=0$ at $N=100$, the matched-seed differences are 0.5164 in success probability (paired-bootstrap 95\% CI [0.5060, 0.5266]) and 1.870~Mbps in goodput (CI [1.721, 2.006]).

\subsection{Topology is a first-order determinant in the model}
At $N=100,R=1,G=0$, sequential pairing produces a 517.20~m mean desired-link distance, whereas greedy short-link pairing reduces it to 84.05~m. Mean SINR changes from $-23.03$ to $-1.75$~dB, first-TX success probability from 0.0128 to 0.4766, and goodput from 0.359 to 15.116~Mbps. Fig.~\ref{fig:topology} shows that the difference persists as $R$ increases. Thus, the severe degradation of the sequential baseline is not a topology-independent density law.

The same effect appears across swarm size. With $R=1,G=0$, sequential pairing keeps mean desired-link distance near 515--525~m from $N=20$ to 100, while success probability falls from 0.0531 to 0.0128. Greedy short-link pairing reduces mean desired-link distance from 195.3~m at $N=20$ to 84.0~m at $N=100$; success probability changes from 0.5834 to 0.4766 despite the increasing number of simultaneous links (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology_scaling}). The experiment therefore separates two competing geometric effects: more active transmitters increase interference, while denser local pairing strengthens the desired link.

\subsection{Allocator and link-adaptation controls}
Resource coordination adds benefit beyond increasing $R$. At $N=100,R=8,G=0$, sequential pairing gives success/goodput 0.2049/1.042~Mbps with the conflict graph versus 0.1463/0.815~Mbps with random allocation. Under greedy short-link pairing, the corresponding values are 0.9465/10.836 and 0.8824/8.357~Mbps. Link adaptation is also material: at $N=100,R=8,G=6$ under sequential pairing, adaptive MCS gives 0.5292/2.229~Mbps versus 0.3309/0.722~Mbps for fixed MCS-4. The adaptive result is therefore an optimistic system-level control case, not normative 3GPP AMC. By contrast, changing from nominal-channel PRB-share noise bandwidth to exact occupied PRB bandwidth changes SINR by at most 0.113~dB over the overlapping robustness grid, with negligible success/goodput change.

\subsection{Mean-based policy operating envelope}
For the explicit policy requiring scenario-mean first-TX success probability $\geq0.10$ and mean expected goodput $\geq1$~Mbps, Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope} reports the largest \emph{evaluated} $N$ meeting both point-estimate thresholds for each $(R,G)$. The sequential baseline reaches $N=10$; several resource/directionality combinations reach the evaluated $N=100$ boundary. These points are policy-qualified operating points, not confidence-bound guarantees or universal capacity limits.
